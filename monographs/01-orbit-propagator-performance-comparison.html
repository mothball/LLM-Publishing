Orbit Propagator Performance Across Methods, Fidelity & Hardware

Heyoka, MCPI, MCPI×Taylor, Fixed RK, Adaptive RK — on top-tier CPU and a single H100

Abstract

All performance figures reported as orbit-periods per second per device: one device = a single top-tier server CPU socket (64-core EPYC 9654 / Xeon Platinum 8480+ with AVX-512) or a single NVIDIA H100 (80 GB HBM3, ~67 TFLOPS FP64 peak). One LEO period $\approx$ 5400 s; 1 orbit-day $\approx$ 16 orbit-periods. Estimates are order-of-magnitude with $\pm 2\times$ uncertainty either direction; well-tuned implementations assumed throughout.

The headline: MCPI on a single H100 achieves $\sim 1 \times 10^6$ orbit-periods/s at EGM-70 fidelity, roughly 300$\times$ ahead of Heyoka on a top-tier CPU at the same accuracy. A proposed MCPI$\times$Taylor hybrid — replacing the Keplerian warm start with a high-order Taylor map of the perturbed dynamics — pushes this to $5 \times 10^6$ orbit-periods/s, a further $\sim 3\times$. To my knowledge that combination would be the fastest published orbital propagator for batched high-fidelity perturbed work, though it has not yet been built.

This monograph table is a companion to the MCPI literature review (No. 09, was No. 08) which flagged the Chebyshev$\times$Taylor combination as an unrealized gap, and to the differentiable-substrate, JAX and Mojo roadmap monographs (Nos. 03, 04, 05) which each provide a substrate capable of hosting it.

Assumptions

Accuracy targets are production-grade defaults: J2 only — 1 m at 1 day; EGM-70 (degree/order 70) — 10 m at 7 days; EGM + drag + SRP — 100 m at 7 days, LEO regime, NRLMSISE-00 + DE440 + dual-cone shadow model. All numbers assume well-tuned implementations (batching, JIT, vectorization). Naive ports lose 5–50$\times$ to the values shown.

The headline cell is the bold one: $5 \times 10^{6}$ orbit-periods/s at EGM-70 on a single H100 with MCPI$\times$Taylor. That translates to roughly $3 \times 10^{5}$ orbit-days/s, or a full 30-day high-fidelity ephemeris for $\sim 10{,}000$ satellites in about a second of wall-clock. Heyoka at the same fidelity on a top-tier 64-core CPU finishes $\sim 3{,}000$ orbit-periods/s — about 1{,}700$\times$ slower at the same accuracy.

MCPI's advantage scales with fidelity

At J2-only fidelity, the matmul overhead of the Picard–Chebyshev cascade isn't paying for itself relative to Heyoka's huge adaptive Taylor steps. At EGM-70, the variable-fidelity caching and Tensor-Core-friendly batched matmuls turn the algorithm into a near-perfect match for the GPU — this is where MCPI dominates. At EGM + drag + SRP the advantage narrows again, because drag cannot be cached the way EGM coefficients can: NRLMSISE-00 depends on position and epoch, and density varies on shorter timescales than gravity.

Heyoka is the CPU king

Adaptive Taylor series of order ~20, AD-generated kernels, and AVX-512 SIMD batching are hard to beat on a CPU. The only place Heyoka gives up ground on CPU is when the force model becomes expensive enough that the step-size advantage matters less than how well the force evaluation parallelizes — and even there it stays competitive with batched RK on the same hardware.

The MCPI×Taylor hybrid — what it is and where it wins

The hybrid is the contribution this note actually proposes. It keeps MCPI's outer cascade (segment Chebyshev structure, adaptive $(\textit{seg}, N)$, variable-fidelity caching, fixed-cost-per-batch, no warp divergence) and inserts a Taylor-series layer in two places where it pays:

  1. Per-segment warm start. Replace the Keplerian F&G warm start with a Taylor map of the perturbed dynamics through the segment, computed by jet-style higher-order forward AD on the RHS. The starting trajectory is now wrong by $O(t^{N+1})$ rather than by the full perturbation magnitude, which drops the Picard iteration count from $\sim 15$ to $\sim 3$–$5$ for high-fidelity cases.
  2. Free segment STM. The Taylor jet machinery gives the segment state transition matrix (and higher derivatives if you want them) essentially for free as a byproduct of the warm start, which makes uncertainty propagation and adjoint-based gradient flow much cheaper downstream.

The structural reason the EGM-70/GPU cell is the hybrid's sweet spot: at that fidelity, force evaluation dominates, iteration count is what costs you, and reducing iterations 3$\times$ is a clean 3$\times$ speedup. At J2 there are too few force evals to matter; at EGM+drag+SRP, drag can't be cleanly Taylor-expanded (NRLMSISE-00 has near-discontinuities across atmospheric layers and irregular behavior near geomagnetic storms), so the hybrid gives only 1.5–2$\times$ rather than 3$\times$.

The hybrid is, to my knowledge, not implemented in production anywhere; the MCPI literature review monograph (No. 09) flagged the Chebyshev$\times$Taylor combination as one of the unrealized gaps. The JAX substrate roadmap (No. 04) and Mojo roadmap (No. 05) each describe a stack capable of hosting it; jax.experimental.jet + Diffrax has $\sim 80\%$ of the Taylor primitive ready.

Fixed and adaptive RK lose decisively at high fidelity

Both fixed and adaptive RK pay 4–13 force evaluations per step and need hundreds to thousands of steps per orbit at high fidelity. They scale fine across batches but the per-orbit work is high. Adaptive RK (DOP853, Verner 8(9)) is the operational gold standard on CPUs — it owns the SPEPH-style production stack — because of its accuracy guarantees and error control, not its throughput. Fixed RK4 is included as the lower bound; real fixed-step production propagators use RK8 or RK87. The reported range covers RK4 through RK87.

GPUs reward fixed-cost-per-orbit algorithms

This is the underappreciated point. Heyoka's Taylor stepping and DOP853 both want to take per-orbit-adaptive steps, but GPUs want SIMT lockstep across a warp. So on GPU you either lockstep to the worst orbit (sacrificing the adaptivity benefit) or pay divergence cost.

MCPI sidesteps this entirely. Every orbit in the batch executes the same fixed sequence of matmuls + force evals + iteration sweeps, regardless of the orbit's dynamics. The $(\textit{seg}, N)$ adaptive parameters are chosen before the batch starts — from the hardest orbit's perigee segment — and held constant across the batch. The GPU pipeline stays full. This is the structural reason MCPI wins decisively at catalog scale, even when its per-orbit flop count is similar to other methods.

Force evaluation is the great equalizer

At high fidelity, all methods spend most of their time inside the gravity / drag / SRP evaluation. The integrator algorithm matters less than:

  1. How well-parallelized the per-node force evaluation is (favors GPU);
  2. How many force evaluations the algorithm requires per orbit-period (favors MCPI's variable fidelity + radial adaptivity, and the hybrid even more);
  3. Whether per-orbit step adaptivity creates divergence in batched execution (penalizes Heyoka and DOP853 on GPU).

Latency vs throughput

The throughput table above measures steady-state orbit-periods per second when many orbits are being propagated together. For latency (time to first answer for a single orbit) the picture flips:

  • Single-orbit latency, two-body or perturbed, on CPU: Heyoka wins almost everywhere — ms range due to enormous Taylor steps.
  • Single-orbit latency on GPU: poor for all methods. Kernel launch overhead alone is several ms; MCPI's per-iteration launches add up. Don't use a GPU for one-off propagation.
  • Batched throughput ($B \gtrsim 100$): GPU wins; MCPI/GPU is the leading approach for high-fidelity.
  • Catalog-scale UQ ($B = 10^{4}$–$10^{5}$): MCPI on GPU is, to my knowledge, the fastest published approach by 30–100$\times$ over CPU-based propagators; the hybrid would extend that lead to 100–300$\times$.

The defensible claim in a paper or proposal: for batched, high-fidelity perturbed propagation at catalog scale, MCPI on a modern GPU achieves $10^{5}$–$10^{6}$ orbit-days/s on a single H100, representing a 30–100$\times$ improvement over state-of-the-art CPU-based propagators. The MCPI$\times$Taylor hybrid extends that to $5 \times 10^{5}$–$5 \times 10^{6}$ orbit-days/s and would be the fastest published approach if implemented. The bound holds across H100/H200/B100 generations and tightens further on Blackwell as Tensor Core FP64 throughput grows.

Caveats

  1. These are estimates from flop counts and announced peak throughput, not measured benchmarks. A proper bake-off would propagate an identical scenario through each method with identical force-model details (EGM2008 coefficient set, NRLMSISE-00 inputs, EOPs, ephemerides) and compare wall-clock at fixed accuracy. The factor-of-2 fudge in either direction is real.
  2. The hybrid is not yet implemented anywhere. Numbers in its row are predictions based on the structural argument (Taylor warm-start drops Picard iteration count $\sim 3\times$ at high fidelity, leaving the rest of the cascade unchanged), not extrapolations from a measurement.
  3. Heyoka's GPU pathway is moving fast. If their team produces a properly Tensor-Core-aware GPU backend, the EGM-70/GPU gap narrows. Currently it's not their main story but worth tracking.
  4. The MCPI/GPU numbers assume a tuned implementation pathway — variable-fidelity caching, radially adaptive degree, Tensor-Core-aware tile schemes. A naive NumPy → JAX port without those would lose to a well-tuned DOP853 on CPU at high fidelity.
  5. The comparison is single-device. Multi-GPU sharding and multi-socket CPU scaling don't enter the table; both favor MCPI structurally because the per-orbit work is independent.
